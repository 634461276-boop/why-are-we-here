# why-are-we-here —— Medium, Perception, and Human Agency in the Age of Machines

**Introduction**

With the rapid advancement of generative AI and automation, machines are increasingly capable of fulfilling our demands on the fly. With just a few words, systems can generate astonishing content on demand. Yet a fundamental question arises: "Why are we here?" Why should we invest time and effort in practicing and learning things that machines are destined to do better in the future?

**Body Paragraph**

The answer is first hidden in the machine's awe-inspiring generative power and the communication gap that accompanies it. While learning to use Midjourney, I deeply experienced how technology can reshape one's perception of "possibility." Its generative capabilities are so immensely powerful that it can instantly visualize highly abstract concepts, shattering my previous boundaries of visual imagination. However, this immense power is coupled with a profound sense of friction - the vast gap between description and specification.

During a recent advertising planning project, I attempted to use Midjourney to generate a series of photos featuring a Chinese father and daughter at different stages of their lives. My requirement was explicit: their appearances had to age naturally over time, but under no circumstances could they morph into different people across the age spans. I knew exactly what I wanted--I had a clear, coherent image in my mind, one that my description was meant to capture--but the machine simply could not align its output perfectly with my vision. When confronted with a high-level human mental model such as "character consistency," the machine's massive computational power revealed an untamable randomness; it could not immediately actualize my thoughts. This made me realize: anyone can make a request, but extracting exactly what you want requires the ability to systematically abstract and model constraints.

As Dylan Beattie noted in his talk *The Art of Code*, technology is never a neutral tool - it is a "medium" with its own affordances and limitations. Just as a painter must understand the chemistry of paint and a photographer the physics of light, today we must treat algorithms as our raw material. Realizing this, I entered a protracted tug-of-war. I continuously refined my prompts, adjusted parameter weights, and introduced reference images to intervene. This process was not a magical button press; it was an agonizing cycle of constant debugging. Every new generation was a leap into the unkown, which might ultimately approximate our exact requirements or end in utter failure after hundreds of attempts. This very process of iteration - fraught with both serendipity and failure - forms the modern creator's most profound understanding of their medium.
