# why-are-we-here —— Medium, Perception, and Human Agency in the Age of Machines

**Introduction**

With the rapid advancement of generative AI and automation, machines are increasingly capable of fulfilling our demands on the fly. With just a few words, systems can generate astonishing content on demand. Yet a fundamental question arises: "Why are we here?" Why should we invest time and effort in practicing and learning things that machines are destined to do better in the future?

**Body Paragraph**

The answer is first hidden in the machine's awe-inspiring generative power and the communication gap that accompanies it. While learning to use Midjourney, I deeply experienced how technology can reshape one's perception of "possibility." Its generative capabilities are so immensely powerful that it can instantly visualize highly abstract concepts, shattering my previous boundaries of visual imagination. However, this immense power is coupled with a profound sense of friction - the vast gap between description and specification.

During a recent advertising planning project, I attempted to use Midjourney to generate a series of photos featuring a Chinese father and daughter at different stages of their lives. My requirement was explicit: their appearances had to age naturally over time, but under no circumstances could they morph into different people across the age spans. I knew exactly what I wanted--I had a clear, coherent image in my mind, one that my description was meant to capture--but the machine simply could not align its output perfectly with my vision. When confronted with a high-level human mental model such as "character consistency," the machine's massive computational power revealed an untamable randomness; it could not immediately actualize my thoughts. This made me realize: anyone can make a request, but extracting exactly what you want requires the ability to systematically abstract and model constraints.

As Dylan Beattie noted in his talk *The Art of Code*, technology is never a neutral tool-it is a "medium" with its own affordances and limitations. Just as a painter must understand the chemistry of paint and a photographer the physics of light, today we must treat algorithms as our raw material. Realizing this, I found myself in a protracted tug-of-war with the machine. I continuously refined my prompts, adjusted parameter weights, and introduced reference images to intervene in its output. This process was not a magical button press; it was an agonizing cycle of constant debugging. Every new generation was a leap into the unknown-one that might approximate our exact requirements or, after hundreds of attempts， end in utter failure. This very process of iteration-fraught with both serendipity and failure-forges the modern creator's most profound understanding of their medium.

Through this process, the human role is fundamentally redefined: we are transitioning from pure "illustrators" or "writers" to "readers and curators." AS machines take over the assembly of pixels, society still requires someone to judge whether the output is aesthetically sound and fulfills the core intent. While repeatedly tweaking prompts, a philosophical question emerged: if the system produces the image, whose artwork is it? The answer is that when you profoundly understand how a system operates and apply precise guidance, you are not a mere bystander-you are the cartographer of the entire "possibility space." The constraints you set and the taste you bring constitute an irreplaceable signature of authorship.

**Conclusion**

This brings us to an uncomfortable reality: the specific tools and prompt syntaxes we painstakingly study-even the current iteration of Midjourney-will likely become obsolete in a few years. Accepting this truth sets us free. We are not here to accumulate depreciating static knowledge. We learn, practice, and wrestle with these tools to cultivate systems thinking, critical intuition, and the pure experience of asserting the human soul within the delicate balance between failure and success.
